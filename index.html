  • Anthropic 分享更多关于 Claude 新水印工作原理的细节
    Anthropic 发布了一篇详细的博客文章，解释了其应用于聊天机器人 Claude 生成文本的水印背后的技术机制。此举紧随该公司早前宣布将实施这些措施，以符合欧盟《人工智能法案》的透明度准则，该准则要求人工智能公司使用能够识别人工智能生成内容的系统。这一公告在 Claude 用户中引发了重大争议，一些 Reddit 用户将该举措描述为针对无辜用户的阴谋，而另一些人则认为，避免这种透明度的唯一理由就是欺骗人们。《商业内幕》报道，数十名 X 平台用户声称因这一消息而取消了他们的 Claude 订阅。 在其新文章中，Anthropic 澄清，水印过程在“低风险选择”期间运行，例如在“overcast”（阴天）和“grey”（灰色）等同义词之间进行选择。这创造了一种人类读者无法察觉但拥有特定解码密钥的人可以识别的模式。该公司强调，这一过程不会影响 Claude 输出的质量，并表示带水印的回复对于读者来说与不带水印的回复无法区分。在技术层面，Anthropic 确认将采用 Google DeepMind 团队在 2024 年概述的 SynthID-Text 方法，并计划发布一个水印检测 API。该公司将这种方法与 Pangram 等人工智能检测工具区分开来，后者寻找写作中的风格“线索”，并指出检查水印与识别语言模式有着根本的不同。 关于水印的持久性，Anthropic承认，通过编辑有可能移除该信号。然而，该公司表示，轻微的编辑可能无法完全去除水印，而将每个词都替换掉的重写则会将其去除。在这种情况下，Anthropic指出，可以争论该文本是否仍可被描述为AI生成的。对于经过Claude校对或编辑的文本，其可检测性取决于文本的长度和编辑的程度；如果仅经过轻微编辑，几乎所有单词仍由人类撰写，留给水印附着的内容就很少。 该实施还影响代码生成。由于模型必须生成功能性代码，且缺乏在同等有效的选项之间选择的自由，水印在代码中的显著程度将低于其他文本。然而，Anthropic解释说，水印可以应用于涉及任意选择的领域，例如代码注释，但它对实际生成的代码影响微乎其微。最后，Anthropic指出，Claude将不是唯一生成带水印文本的AI聊天机器人，因为其他主要模型开发者已签署相同的《行为准则》，并正在实施他们自己的水印。
    [Original] Anthropic shares more details about how Claude’s new watermarks will work
    Anthropic has published a detailed blog post explaining the technical mechanics behind the watermarks it will apply to text generated by its chatbot, Claude. The move follows the company’s earlier announcement that it would implement these measures to comply with the EU AI Act’s Transparency Code, which mandates that AI companies utilize systems capable of identifying AI-generated content. The announcement has sparked significant debate among Claude users, with some on Reddit characterizing the initiative as a conspiracy against innocent users, while others argued that the only reason to avoid such transparency is to deceive people. Business Insider reported that dozens of users on X have claimed to cancel their Claude subscriptions in response to the news. In its new post, Anthropic clarifies that the watermarking process operates during "low-stakes choices," such as selecting between synonymous words like "overcast" and "grey." This creates a pattern that is undetectable to human readers but identifiable by those possessing the specific decoding key. The company emphasized that this process does not impact the quality of Claude’s output, stating that watermarked responses are indistinguishable from unwatermarked ones to a reader. Technically, Anthropic confirmed it will utilize the SynthID-Text approach outlined by the Google DeepMind team in 2024 and plans to release a watermark detection API. The company distinguished this method from AI detection tools like Pangram, which look for stylistic "tells" in writing, noting that checking for a watermark is fundamentally different from identifying linguistic patterns. Regarding the durability of the watermark, Anthropic acknowledged that it is possible to remove the signal through editing. However, the company stated that light editing will likely not remove the watermark completely, whereas a complete rewrite replacing every word will. In such a case, Anthropic noted it is arguable whether the text can still be described as AI-generated. For text that has been proofread or edited by Claude, detectability depends on the length of the text and the extent of the edits; if only lightly edited, nearly all words remain human-authored, leaving little for the watermark to attach to. The implementation also affects code generation. Because models must produce functional code and lack the freedom to choose between equally valid options, the watermark will be less prominent in code than in other text. However, Anthropic explained that the watermark can be applied in areas involving arbitrary choices, such as code comments, though it will have a negligible effect on the actual code produced. Finally, Anthropic noted that Claude will not be the only AI chatbot generating watermarked text, as other major model developers have signed the same Code of Practice and are implementing their own watermarks.

    Neuroscientist and former teacher Jared Cooney Horvath argues that the widespread integration of educational technology in schools is undermining children’s cognitive development, a claim he details in his book, *The Digital Delusion*. He identifies the “meteoric rise of educational technology” as the primary cause of declining IQ scores and international test results that began around 2000, even as time spent in school increased. The financial scale of this shift is substantial. A recent Department for Education report indicates the UK educational technology market generates an annual turnover of approximately £6.5bn, while in the US, Horvath describes it as a $400bn (£295bn) mega-industry woven into every aspect of schooling. Horvath notes that while laptop uptake began in earnest in 2010-12, the pandemic forced a rapid digital pivot that schools have largely maintained. In the US, 88% of school districts now have one-to-one computers, and the UK experienced a similar trajectory. Horvath advocates for reducing screen use in academic settings, stating, “It’s about reducing them as much as possible for academic learning as well if we want our kids to have deep transferrable knowledge in our heads.” Supporting his argument, Horvath cites data from the Programme for International Student Assessment (Pisa), which tracks the academic performance of 15-year-olds. Analysis of 2012, 2015, and 2018 data suggests a negative correlation between screen time and performance: students using computers for more than six hours per day scored 66 points lower than peers who did not use them at all. However, experts caution against drawing definitive causal links from this correlational data. Rose Luckin, an emerita professor at the UCL Knowledge Lab, notes that while much edtech was sold on promises rather than proof, “moderate school device use is associated with better results than no school device use,” with only the heaviest daily use showing negative outcomes. Daisy Christodoulou, director of education at No More Marking, agrees that society has been “a bit blase” about the impact of new technologies, though she remains more optimistic about technology’s potential in upper secondary education, noting screens have had less impact in England than in the US. Dylan Wiliam, an emeritus professor at the UCL Institute of Education, emphasizes that current correlational studies cannot support causal claims that technology is harming achievement. He points to well-designed studies showing positive impacts, concluding that the question of whether edtech negatively impacts learning does not have a simple answer.     Turning pubs into housing or offices in England will become significantly more difficult under updated government planning rules, as the revised National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) takes effect on Monday. The new regulations are designed to provide stronger protections for pubs facing closure and conversion to other uses. While a previous consultation limited protections to the last remaining pub in an area, the updated framework requires developers seeking to change a pub’s use to provide evidence that there is no reasonable prospect of keeping the business running. This evidence must include proof that the establishment was marketed for sale for at least 12 months. A government spokesperson stated that local councils will be required to assess the community impact of such changes, and the rules will reportedly prevent owners from deliberately undermining trade to justify closure, thereby protecting historically successful establishments. The NPPF outlines planning policies for England, which are applied by local authorities, while Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland maintain their own devolved frameworks. Industry groups have long campaigned against the loss of pubs, with the Campaign for Real Ale warning that pubs are being "lost forever to conversion or demolition" as developers exploit their desirable locations and unique architecture. However, the British Beer and Pub Association and UKHospitality argue that rising taxes and employment costs are the primary drivers of closures. Allen Simpson, chief executive of UKHospitality, stated, "The biggest issue facing hospitality businesses is costs like VAT and business rates pushing pubs out of business in the first place." He added that while making it harder to remove these assets from communities is welcome, the government should focus on fixing the harm done to the hospitality sector over the past two years. In July, the Labour government announced a 20% cut to business rates for pubs, social clubs, and live music venues in England, supplementing earlier rates relief. The NPPF update also includes measures to boost housebuilding, such as default approval for homes built around railway stations in England. Housing Secretary Angela Rayner said, "By unlocking thousands of homes around well-connected transport hubs, we're helping people live closer to work, school and the services they rely on, while backing local businesses and driving growth in our communities." The changes also mean some groups will no longer be consulted on housing development plans. The Gardens Trust, a heritage charity, called the removal of its role as a statutory consultee "devastating," arguing it devalues parks and gardens and makes them "vulnerable to poorly informed development." Although the Gardens Trust and Theatres Trust will still be notified of relevant applications, and Sport England will continue to advise on significant cases, the shift has drawn criticism. Labour has set a target to build 1.5 million homes by 2029. Official data indicates that 392,000 homes have been built in England since the party came into power in July 2024.
