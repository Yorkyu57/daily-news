    [Original] Meta and Google trial: are infinite scroll and autoplay creating addicts?
    This week, a landmark social media harm case against Meta and Google concluded its six-week trial in Los Angeles with closing arguments, drawing comparisons to the tobacco litigation of the 1990s. Prosecuting attorney Mark Lanier asserted it was "easy as ABC" to prove the defendants guilty of "addicting the brains of children." Conversely, Meta defended its record, stating that providing a "safer, healthier experience has always been core to our work." Central to the trial were platform features such as infinite scrolling, autoplay videos, and constant alerts, which critics argue are engineered to create dependency. Arturo Béjar, a former Meta child safety employee and whistleblower, explained the mechanics of infinite scroll: "There is always something more that will give you another dopamine hit that you react to and there is an infinite supply of that." He noted the design promises a "never-ending supply" of rewarding content. Internal documents revealed employee concerns regarding rising "reward tolerance," with one 2020 email describing Instagram as a "drug" and colleagues admitting, "We're basically pushers." Béjar emphasized that users are "constantly chasing" content with "no bounds on that part of the mechanism." Sonia Livingstone, a professor at the London School of Economics, observed that young people make "split-second decisions to swipe, swipe, swipe," driven by the feeling that "the next thing could be good." Regarding autoplay, Béjar noted that while consumers initially "hated it" as "disruptive," it successfully increased video consumption and advertiser revenue by triggering a human reaction to "watch enough to understand what is going on." Lanier likened these features to free tortilla chips that users cannot stop eating. Notifications and likes further drive engagement. Mark Griffith, a professor of behavioral addiction, explained that winning the competition for likes provides a "little hit of enjoyment," releasing dopamine and adrenaline. While he acknowledged that for some, social media is "genuinely addictive," he clarified it does not meet the criteria for addiction to substances like nicotine or cocaine for most. Instead, he described a "moreish quality," distinguishing between "habitual use" affecting productivity and "problematic use" with serious implications. Instagram chief executive Adam Mosseri testified that social media is not "clinically addictive." He argued that while people can be addicted to a "good television show," this differs from clinical addiction. Jurors began deliberations on Friday, with their verdict expected to closely redefine tech companies' responsibilities regarding platform design. The outcome will determine whether features woven into the fabric of these platforms constitute a public health crisis or simply consumer preference.

    [Original] Fake rooms, props and a script to lure victims: inside an abandoned Cambodia scam centre
    A sprawling, industrial-scale cybercrime compound in O'Smach, a Cambodian border town, has been exposed following its seizure by the Thai military during last year's border clashes. The site, now under Thai control despite Cambodian protests labeling the presence "unacceptable and unlawful," reveals an elaborate network of fraud operations targeting victims across Asia, Australia, and South America. The Guardian was granted access to the facility, which features six floors of meticulously designed "mock-up" rooms, including fake branches of Vietnam's OCB bank and offices mimicking police forces from Australia, Singapore, China, and Brazil. Inside, investigators found props such as fake uniforms, badges, scripts, and stacks of counterfeit $100 bills left behind as workers fled the escalating conflict. The buildings, riddled with bullet holes and debris, contain rooms padded with noise-cancelling foam for up to 30 workers each. Whiteboards tracked financial targets, with one note reading, "No money, already took 279k." A notebook targeting Brazilian victims detailed daily strategies: "For today there is no customer [who] will transfer the money to us – they are trying to grab new customer and talk with old." Documents recovered by Thai authorities outline sophisticated tactics, including romance scams and impersonation of law enforcement. One guide instructed workers to "Create a touching family story... to encourage clients to spontaneously express concern and empathy," while another suggested using phrases like "I think I'm getting used to talking to you every day." Scripts directed workers to call Indian victims regarding "illegal advertising," Australian restaurant owners about meal orders, and Singaporean individuals accused of money laundering. Training materials included quizzes identifying ideal targets as "Male," "North American," and aged "40-65." Pol Maj Gen Siriwat Deepor of the Royal Thai police described the operation as company-like, featuring training, finance, and creative departments. Thai defence ministry spokesperson Surasant Kongsiri estimated 20,000 workers were based across the wider complex of 157 buildings, with 28 suspected of scamming. Online fraud in Cambodia is estimated to generate $12.5bn annually, half the country's formal GDP, according to the United States Institute of Peace. Following international pressure and sanctions against alleged kingpin Chen Zhi, who was extradited to China in January, Cambodia pledged to eliminate scamming by April.
