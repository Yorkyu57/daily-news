Nearby, neighborhood boys joined municipal workers clearing rubble from a children’s activity center, where painted purple butterflies remained visible on broken windows. Away from the blast epicenter, normality returned. Around the corner, children played on a swing, watching the bustle in disbelief. Further off, road workers laid new tarmac and buses ran as if nothing unusual had occurred. This is Kyiv’s way of coping with war: no matter how hard it is hit, the city returns to its daily routines.

    [Original] Microsoft says new quantum chip 1,000 times more reliable than predecessor
    Microsoft has announced a major breakthrough in quantum computing with its new Majorana 2 chip, claiming it is vastly more reliable than its predecessor and could lead to a commercially useful quantum computer within three years. The chip’s qubits—the fundamental units of quantum computation—now survive for an average of 20 seconds, compared to milliseconds for the Majorana 1, representing a 1,000-fold improvement in reliability. Microsoft compares this leap to the difference between a phone needing daily charging and one requiring a charge every few years. "We will have a quantum machine in 2029 that can solve commercially viable, reasonable problems," said Zulfi Alam, corporate vice president of Microsoft Quantum. However, the current chip contains only 12 qubits, and a functional quantum computer would require millions. Microsoft does not release full technical details publicly, citing commercial confidentiality. The company has spent 20 years pursuing a "topological" approach to quantum computing, based on exploiting a quasi-particle first theorized in the 1930s by Italian physicist Ettore Majorana. This required creating a novel state of matter beyond solid, liquid, or gas. The approach has been controversial: Microsoft retracted a 2018 Nature paper claiming evidence for the Majorana particle, and experts have expressed skepticism. Henry Legg, a physicist at the University of St Andrews, previously told the BBC that Microsoft’s research had "moved firmly away from science and entered the realm of faith." Despite this, Microsoft stands by its work. "We really look to scientific rigor. We welcome the debate," said Jason Zander, executive vice president of Microsoft Quantum and Discovery. The company is part of the final stage of a quantum development program run by the U.S. defense research agency Darpa, which aims to verify Microsoft’s utility-scale quantum computer concept. Microsoft says it has shared all data, including commercially sensitive material, with Darpa for assessment. However, a paper published alongside the announcement has not been peer-reviewed. The Majorana 2 chip is based on the same principles as its predecessor but is more effective partly because scientists replaced aluminium with lead as a superconductor—a human insight, not an AI discovery. Microsoft is also using AI to accelerate its research. Paul Stevenson, a physics professor at the University of Surrey, called the timeline plausible if the research holds up. "Microsoft appears to have made a leap... If they succeed, they will leap from being a player with no production quantum computer, to being a serious player," he said. Microsoft envisions quantum computers tackling problems like eliminating microplastics or developing better fertilizers, potentially compressing decades of research into a shorter timeframe. "It’s not about eliminating humans, it’s about giving humans tools that can help them accelerate that process," Zander said. However, qubits remain extremely fragile, and building a scalable quantum computer remains an unachieved goal across the industry.
