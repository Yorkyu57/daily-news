    [Original] FDA celebrates progress to end animal testing but experts warn there’s still a long way to go
    Twelve months after releasing a roadmap to reduce animal testing requirements, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) declared on April 19, 2026, that it has achieved its first-year goals. FDA Commissioner Marty Makary, M.D., stated in a release shared with Fierce Biotech that the agency has made significant strides in replacing animal testing with more precise methods to predict human drug safety. Makary noted that these improvements aim to lower research and development costs, ultimately reducing drug prices for Americans. The FDA highlighted that over 90% of drugs clearing animal studies fail to receive approval, a statistic the agency hopes to rectify through New Approach Methodologies (NAMs). Key initiatives include a searchable database for NAM applications, a qualified AI tool for analyzing liver images, and draft guidance limiting primate testing for monoclonal antibodies while accepting unvalidated NAM data. The FDA's 2025 roadmap targets making animal testing the exception rather than the norm in preclinical development by 2030, potentially as early as 2028. Future efforts will focus on expanding NAM initiatives beyond antibodies, tracking animal use changes, and validating methods for critical drug development endpoints. However, experts caution that the path to meaningful animal removal remains long. Matthew Bailey, President of the National Association for Biomedical Research (NABR), supported the FDA's goals but provided context, arguing that both animal models and NAMs are surrogates of human disease where the "context of use determines the choice." Bailey challenged the FDA's 90% failure rate claim, citing Biotechnology Innovation Organization data showing only 9.6% of drugs tested in humans are approved, suggesting animal study success rates are comparable to human clinical trials. He called for success rate data on NAM-only development and emphasized that patient safety must remain paramount, noting we are far from wholesale replacement of living systems. Steven Bulera, Ph.D., Chief Scientific Officer at Charles River Laboratories, described the transition as "evolutionary rather than revolutionary," applauding the FDA's commitment while noting the complexity of the shift. The draft guidance allowing unvalidated NAM data, such as organoids and organ-on-chips, received mixed reactions. Natalie Ma, Ph.D., of Deep Origin, praised the guidance for providing "real examples" and "concrete categories," noting that computational predictions can now build a track record. Conversely, Vanda Pharmaceuticals criticized the guidance for lacking clear examples of accepted NAMs, scant scientific references, and vague validation requirements. Vanda President Mihael Polymeropoulos, M.D., urged the FDA to withdraw the draft and replace it with stronger scientific grounding. Bailey concluded by questioning public willingness to volunteer for human trials based solely on NAM testing, suggesting most would currently decline.

    [Original] Tehran will never cede control of Strait of Hormuz, senior Iranian politician tells BBC
    Senior Iranian lawmaker Ebrahim Azizi has declared that Iran will "never" relinquish control of the Strait of Hormuz, describing it as an "inalienable right." Azizi, a former Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps (IRGC) commander and head of the parliament's Committee for National Security and Foreign Policy, stated that Tehran is introducing a bill based on Article 110 of the constitution to enshrine this authority in law. The legislation, covering environment, maritime safety, and national security, will be implemented by the armed forces, granting Iran the power to decide passage rights for all vessels. As global economic anxiety mounts regarding the potential closure of this strategic waterway, Azizi characterized the strait as a weaponized asset and "one of our assets to face the enemy." This stance reflects a shift toward a militarized order dominated by hardliners following high-level assassinations in Israeli strikes. Mohammad Eslami, a research fellow at the University of Tehran, noted that restoring deterrence is Iran's primary post-war priority, with the strait serving as a principal strategic leverage. While Tehran claims openness to discussing benefits for other nations, Azizi emphasized that "control is the bottom line." Regional neighbors have strongly rejected this vision. Dr. Anwar Gargash, diplomatic adviser to the UAE president, labeled the move an "act of hostile piracy" that would set a "dangerous precedent." Azizi retorted by calling the UAE "pirates who sold our region to the Americans" and identified the US as "the biggest pirate in the world." While Oman, a close ally controlling the strait's southern coast, has engaged in talks to ensure safe transit, most Gulf states view Iran's vision as shattered. Internal disagreements within Iran's elite surfaced when Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi posted that the strait was "completely open," prompting a "THANK YOU" from US President Donald Trump. IRGC-linked outlets immediately rebuked Araghchi, with Mehr news agency claiming the post allowed Trump to "declare himself the winner," and Tasnim calling it a "bad and incomplete tweet." Araghchi later clarified that the waterway remains open only to IRGC-authorized ships on designated routes requiring toll payments. Azizi dismissed reports of internal rifts, stating, "When it comes to national security, there are no moderate or hardline approaches." The strait's fate remains a core issue in high-level talks set to resume in Islamabad, potentially involving a US delegation led by Vice-President JD Vance. Iranian officials have remained silent on their attendance, with local media suggesting they will not return while a US blockade persists. Trump has accused Tehran of "blackmail," warning Iran would be "living in hell" if it failed to comply. Azizi dismissed these claims as American blackmail, asserting Iran is merely defending its rights. Regarding the near-total digital blackout and recent executions of protesters, Azizi blamed US and Israeli spy agencies for the unrest and insisted that "in war, even in a ceasefire, there are rules."

Customers were instructed not to consume HiPP jars purchased at Eurospar, Interspar, or Maximarkt and to return them for refunds. With over 1,500 Spar shops in Austria, police emphasized that baby food sold in other retailers remains unaffected, as does HiPP's baby formula. In January and February, Nestle and Danone recalled infant formula in more than 60 countries, including the UK, after babies fell ill.
