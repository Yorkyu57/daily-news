    A bitter diplomatic dispute between Hungary, Slovakia, and Ukraine is currently blocking a crucial €90bn (£77.95bn) EU loan package intended for Kyiv. The impasse stems from the cessation of oil flow through the Druzhba (Friendship) pipeline, which transports Russian crude to Hungary and Slovakia via Ukraine. No oil has passed through the line since a Russian bombardment on 27 January severely damaged the Brody oil hub in western Ukraine's Lviv region. While Ukraine asserts that repairs require six additional weeks due to the severity of the damage, Budapest accuses Kyiv of stalling as political revenge for Hungary's pro-Russian stance. This conflict highlights how individual member states can obstruct EU decision-making, while simultaneously exposing Hungary and Slovakia to fuel shortages after refusing to wean themselves off Russian oil since 2022. Satellite imagery obtained by the BBC indicates the attack destroyed a massive 75,000 cubic metre storage tank essential for maintaining pipeline pressure. Ukrainian energy expert Henadiy Ryabtsev warns that the fire, which raged for approximately a week, likely damaged pumps and the pipeline itself due to extreme heat. "The oil was on fire for about a week, which means that during that time everything around it was affected by the high temperature," Ryabtsev stated. Ukrainian Foreign Ministry spokesman Heorhy Tykhy described the situation as a technical issue exacerbated by war. "Our infrastructure is being damaged every day... Ukraine repairs all of that, but we do not have unlimited resources. We are in a full-scale war, which Hungary decides to ignore for some reason," Tykhy said. Although President Volodymyry Zelensky previously expressed reluctance to restore the flow of Russian oil, the EU has offered immediate technical support and funding, which Kyiv has accepted. Conversely, Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orban, who maintains close ties with Moscow, claims the delay is political. Zsolt Hernadi, CEO of Hungarian energy company MOL, argued the pipeline remains intact, noting that 35,000 tons of crude flowed through without issue after the attack. "The absence of a decision can hardly be described as a technical issue," Hernadi stated. A MOL-commissioned study suggested the underground pipeline was unlikely to be harmed. However, security analyst András Rácz of the German Council on Foreign Relations disputes this, citing conversations with engineers. "The superheated oil cooked the system from inside, damaging the sensors, the valves, all the safety systems inside the pipeline," Rácz explained. He noted that while the pipe is physically intact, internal components require replacement or verification. Compounding the issue, Ukrainian engineers face nightly air raids and a shortage of experts. Meanwhile, Hungary has begun importing non-Russian Brent crude, but MOL refineries lack the equipment to process this lower-sulphur oil.
