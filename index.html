    [Original] Anthropic’s landmark $1.5B copyright settlement is approved
    A federal judge has granted final approval for Anthropic’s landmark $1.5 billion settlement with a class of authors and book publishers who sued the AI lab for copyright infringement. The approval, issued on Monday by Judge Araceli Martinez-Olguin of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, allows Anthropic to begin distributing payments to the affected rights holders. The settlement resolves a lawsuit originally filed after Judge William Alsup, who has since retired, ruled that Anthropic had illegally downloaded and stored millions of copyrighted books. Alsup had previously issued preliminary approval for the agreement last year. Under the terms of the settlement, Anthropic will pay $3,000 per work across an estimated 500,000 works, with funds shared among the authors and publishers holding the rights. While this payout is believed to be the largest in the history of U.S. copyright law, many creators do not view the outcome as a victory. This sentiment stems from how the core legal question was resolved: Alsup sided with Anthropic on the issue of fair use, ruling that training an AI model on copyrighted text constitutes fair use. This decision is widely regarded as a turning point for the AI industry. However, the judge did not excuse Anthropic’s method of obtaining the books. The company had built its training library from two sources: legally purchased and scanned books, and books downloaded from pirate sites such as Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror. Alsup found the latter method illegal and stated that the piracy question could proceed to trial. Anthropic agreed to the settlement to avoid a trial and potential jury-awarded damages. Although the final approval closes this specific case, it does not settle the legal question industrywide. Alsup’s ruling was a single district court decision, and because Anthropic settled, the case will never reach an appeals court to establish binding precedent. Other judges remain free to reach their own conclusions, a scenario currently unfolding in other jurisdictions. Numerous copyright lawsuits are pending against companies including Google, Meta, Midjourney, and OpenAI regarding the legality of training AI models on copyrighted works. Just last week, a group of publishers and authors, including Hachette, Cengage, Elsevier, author Scott Turow, and S.C.R.I.B.E., filed a class action lawsuit against Google, accusing the company of using their copyrighted works to train its AI platform, Gemini.
