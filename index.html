    [Original] Sanofi gains long-awaited win for tolebrutinib as EU regulator endorses MS drug for approval
    The European Medicines Agency’s Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) recommended approval of Sanofi’s tolebrutinib for patients with secondary progressive multiple sclerosis (SPMS) who have not had relapses in the last two years. The CHMP cited a 31% reduction in disability progression and a 38% reduction in new or enlarging lesions per year compared with placebo. In Europe, the drug will be marketed as Cenrifki. The recommendation marks a victory for Sanofi after a series of regulatory and clinical setbacks. Last year, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) rejected tolebrutinib for the same indication, citing efficacy and safety issues, stating that “a favorable benefit-risk profile could not be established for any patient subpopulation.” Also in December, Sanofi disclosed the failure of a Phase 3 trial for primary progressive MS, ending hopes in that indication, following two other late-stage MS trial failures in 2024. Sanofi acquired the BTK inhibitor through its $3.7 billion purchase of Principia Biopharma in 2020. The FDA has cited the risk of drug-induced liver injury, which it said cannot be adequately managed by Sanofi’s proposed risk evaluation and mitigation strategy. Separately, the CHMP recommended approval of Novartis’ gene therapy Itvisma for spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) in Europe, five months after the FDA approved it. Itvisma is Novartis’ follow-on to Zolgensma, which in 2019 became the first gene therapy for a neuromuscular disease with a $2.1 million price tag. Itvisma is priced at $2.59 million in the U.S., representing a reduction in the cost of standard SMA treatments over 10 years. While the active drug substances are identical, Itvisma is administered via intrathecal injection near the spinal cord, whereas Zolgensma is given intravenously, allowing Itvisma to be given to heavier patients in a more concentrated dose. The CHMP also issued a positive opinion for Arrowhead’s Redemplo, the first small interfering RNA medicine for familial chylomicronemia syndrome. The FDA approved Redemplo five months ago as an adjunct to diet to reduce triglycerides in adults with the rare genetic disorder. Additionally, Novartis withdrew a CHMP application for its radioligand therapy Pluvicto to treat adults with PSMA-positive metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer who have no or mild symptoms after progression despite hormone-blocking therapy. The withdrawal comes 13 months after the FDA expanded Pluvicto’s approval to patients who have not had chemotherapy. Earlier this month, Soleno Therapeutics withdrew its application for Viokat, a treatment for hyperphagia associated with Prader-Willi Syndrome, after the CHMP determined trial data were insufficient to establish that benefits outweighed risks. The FDA approved Viokat in March of last year as the first treatment for hyperphagia.

    [Original] Daiichi pushes back annual report, citing reviews amid ‘rapidly changing business conditions’
    Daiichi Sankyo has postponed its annual report by two weeks, now scheduled for release on May 11 alongside its new five-year business plan, citing a need for additional time to finalize financial figures. The Japanese pharmaceutical company stated in an April 24 announcement that the delay is driven by a review of “the supply plans for its oncology products portfolio and development pipeline in light of rapidly changing business conditions.” This review requires “additional deliberation to reasonably estimate the amount of loss provisions to be recorded in connection with contracts with contract manufacturers.” Following the announcement, Daiichi’s shares on the Tokyo Stock Exchange fell by over 10%. The delay comes weeks after President Donald Trump imposed a 100% tariff on patented drugs and ingredients. Under existing trade deals, products from Japan and the European Union face a 15% tariff, though the administration has indicated openness to exemptions under “most favored nation” drug pricing deals similar to those signed with 17 large pharmaceutical companies. Daiichi operates two in-house manufacturing facilities in the U.S., in Ohio and New York, and is reportedly investing up to 56 billion yen ($351 million) to expand its Ohio campus as part of its global manufacturing expansion for its core antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) business. The Ohio plant handles fill-finish and packaging for ADCs. Ken Keller, head of Daiichi’s U.S. branch and global oncology business, told Fierce in January that the company produces monoclonal antibody components for its ADCs in the U.S. but not the linker parts. “We feel it’s better for us to look at ways to onshore things, but we’re still developing those plans,” Keller said. Daiichi also uses contract development and manufacturing organizations (CDMOs), a strategy that suffered a setback in 2024 when the FDA rejected its Merck & Co.-partnered HER3 ADC, patritumab deruxtecan, due to a contractor’s manufacturing issues. The pair later withdrew the application after a phase 3 miss on overall survival in EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer. Additionally, the ongoing Iran war is disrupting supply chains by raising energy costs and complicating logistics. As of its October 2025 earnings update, Daiichi expected annual revenue of 2,100 billion yen ($13.1 billion), an 11.3% increase over the previous fiscal year. By 2030, the company aims to have at least four ADCs on the market: AstraZeneca-partnered Enhertu and Datroway, plus HER3-DXd and Merck-partnered CDH6-directed raludotatug deruxtecan.     [Original] ‘Opening the hidden door within us’: how Exit 8 took a simple game to purgatory
    Genki Kawamura, a bestselling author, filmmaker, and lifelong gamer, has adapted the Japanese horror game *Exit 8* into a film now in cinemas. The original game, developed by a lone Kyoto-based coder known as Kotake Create, traps players in an endlessly looping Tokyo subway station corridor. Players must spot anomalies—such as a poster’s eyes following them or a commuter smiling—to escape through the titular Exit 8 after eight successful runs. “I was captivated by its game design and the beauty of its visuals,” Kawamura said. He noted that while the game is simple, each player creates their own story, calling it “a device that could reveal something fundamental about human nature.” For the film, Kawamura expanded the experience with a fuller narrative. It begins with a commuter on a packed train who ignores a crying baby being berated by another passenger. After alighting, his ex-girlfriend calls to say she is pregnant, prompting an asthma attack that leaves him trapped in a deserted, inescapable subway tunnel. The ensuing psychological thriller, described as part *Groundhog Day* and part *Vivarium*, features a stalking silent commuter, dripping blood, flickering lights, and reconfiguring tunnels. “The starting point was my daily commute on the Tokyo subway,” Kawamura said. “On a packed train, everyone is absorbed in their smartphones… Even when a baby is crying right in front of them, they don’t notice. Our phones are filled with images of war and violence, and yet we scroll past them… I think this small sense of guilt… quietly accumulates inside all of us.” Kawamura envisioned the corridor as a purgatory, inspired by the *Divine Comedy*. “The yellow Exit 8 sign was designed as a godlike presence, something that governs the space and watches over humans as they confront their sins,” he explained. The game’s rule—“If you notice something strange, turn back; if not, keep going”—mirrors life’s constant choices and became the film’s central narrative pillar. *Exit 8* is part of a trend of indie horror games based on featureless corridors and liminal spaces, stemming from the “Backrooms” internet mythology born on 4chan in 2019. Kawamura noted that liminal spaces “feel like being inside a nightmare” and “pull out the deepest fears buried in human memory.” He added, “More than external threats like monsters or ghosts, what’s truly terrifying is opening the hidden door within ourselves. And I think people today are increasingly drawn to that ambiguity itself.”

    [Original] China car giant BYD says it can thrive without US
    The recent surge in fuel prices due to the war in Iran has spurred global demand for electric vehicles, and Chinese car makers are capitalizing on the opportunity. China is the world’s top EV producer, and while its manufacturers remain largely shut out of the U.S. market, they are benefiting from increased orders across Asia and elsewhere. BYD, which overtook Tesla as the world’s largest EV seller last year and is expanding aggressively overseas, is at the center of this shift. “We survive and are successful without the US market today,” BYD executive vice president Stella Li told the BBC at the Beijing Auto Show. Instead of targeting U.S. customers, the company says its challenge is meeting demand in regions including Brazil, the UK, and Europe. “Consumers feel the daily savings when oil prices increase. EVs help them save money every day,” Li said. “Actually, we are now suffering [insufficient] capacity. Our demand is much higher than what we can supply.” BYD is betting on its new “flash charging” technology, which Li describes as a “game-changer” to overcome concerns over charging speeds. Flash charging can add hundreds of kilometres of range in minutes, potentially persuading reluctant customers to consider an EV. At this year’s Beijing Auto Show, now the world’s largest industry event, more than 1,400 vehicles from hundreds of companies were displayed, with Chinese carmakers centre stage. BYD’s global push unfolds against a complex geopolitical backdrop. Chinese EV-makers face tariffs and regulatory scrutiny in global markets, particularly the U.S., which has criticized Chinese subsidies and raised data protection and national security concerns. But Li said the firm is winning greater brand recognition elsewhere, including the UK. Chinese firms are increasingly competing on technology—batteries, charging infrastructure, and software integration—rather than just price. “We are not just a car company. We produce one-third of global smartphone components, we are a leading player in battery storage, solar panels, buses, and trucks. So BYD is an ecosystem,” Li said. The Auto Show displayed innovations beyond cars. China’s X-Peng unveiled a six-seater electric SUV, with CEO He Xiapoeng saying humanoid robots would follow this year and flying cars in 2027. Foreign carmakers like Volkswagen, Toyota, and Ford, which once dominated China’s market, are struggling and collaborating with local firms. BMW has partnered with battery maker CATL, Audi uses Huawei’s driving assistance systems, and Volkswagen is co-developing EVs with XPeng. Competition within China is intense, with price wars and rapid product cycles. Even for BYD, domestic sales have fallen for seven straight months, contrasting with European sales up 156% in the first quarter of this year. Li said pressure from competition makes consolidation inevitable. “History suggests not all will survive,” she said, citing past cycles with Japanese and South Korean manufacturers.

    [Original] How does it affect me if share prices fall?
    Bank of England Deputy Governor Sarah Breedon has become the latest prominent figure to question whether current share prices are irrationally high, stating she expects them to fall to reflect the numerous risks facing the global economy. While stock market fluctuations often make headlines, many people believe they are unaffected because they do not directly invest. However, millions of individuals with defined contribution pensions—either private or through work—see their savings invested by pension schemes, meaning the value of their pot is directly influenced by market performance. Hundreds of billions of pounds are held in such pensions, so widespread share price falls are likely bad news for savers. Experts urge pension holders not to panic, emphasizing that investments require a long-term outlook and that knee-jerk reactions should be avoided. Timing is especially critical for those nearing retirement, who may use their pension pot to buy an annuity. As retirement approaches, pots are typically moved to less risky investments like government bonds, which can perform better when stocks fall. For those already taking an income from their pot, market declines could mean receiving less than expected if too much is cashed in after a fall. Extended share price drops could also affect jobs, as investors may pressure companies to cut costs. Conversely, lower share prices can present buying opportunities, often through a stocks and shares Individual Savings Account (Isa). Experts stress the importance of diversification and warn that investments can go down as well as up. The government, led by Chancellor Rachel Reeves, is encouraging more people in the UK to invest, arguing it could boost economic growth and noting that investing is more popular in the US and parts of Europe. Reeves has announced Isa reforms for 2027 and is backing an advertising campaign funded by major financial firms. The campaign, fronted by a character named "Savvy the Squirrel," has raised some eyebrows, with questions about whether it can match the success of the 1980s "Tell Sid" adverts for British Gas. Finally, big stock market shifts can also affect currency exchange rates and prices for goods and services, as global economic upheaval and oil price changes impact household costs.

    [Original] Who is making decisions in Iran?
    Since the opening strikes of Iran’s war with the US and Israel on 28 February, a central question has hung over Tehran: Who is in charge? Formally, the answer is Mojtaba Khamenei, who assumed the role of supreme leader after his father, Ali Khamenei, was killed. In the Islamic Republic’s system, the leader holds final authority over war, peace, and strategic direction. In practice, the picture is far murkier. Donald Trump has described Iran’s leadership as “fractured,” suggesting the US awaits a “unified proposal.” Unity was on the minds of Iran’s leaders when they distributed a message to citizens stating there was “no such thing as a hardliner or moderate in Iran – there was just one nation, one course.” Mojtaba Khamenei has not been seen in public since taking power. Beyond a handful of written statements, including one insisting the Strait of Hormuz remains closed, there is little evidence of his day-to-day control. Iranian officials have acknowledged he was injured in the initial strikes. *The New York Times*, citing Iranian sources, reported he may have suffered injuries to his face that have made it difficult for him to speak. That absence matters. In Iran’s system, authority is performative; the late leader signalled intent through speeches and visible arbitration. That function is now largely missing. Decision-making appears less centralised. On paper, diplomacy sits with the government. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi continues to represent Tehran in talks with the US under President Masoud Pezeshkian. But neither appears to set strategy, and their authority is questioned by the fact that Parliament Speaker Mohammad-Bagher Ghalibaf heads the delegation. Araghchi’s role looks operational; his brief reversal over whether Hormuz was open or closed offered a glimpse of how little control the diplomatic track has over military decisions. Pezeshkian has aligned with the regime’s direction without visibly shaping it. Control over the Strait of Hormuz sits with the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), led by Ahmad Vahidi. The IRGC’s actions—enforcing the closure or striking targets across the Gulf—appear to set the pace of the crisis, with political responses often following. This suggests the IRGC’s operational autonomy has widened in the absence of clear political arbitration. Into this ambiguity steps Ghalibaf, a former Guard commander now serving as speaker. He has inserted himself into negotiations and framed the war in pragmatic terms. However, hardline resistance to negotiations remains strong, with state media framing talks as weakness. Ghalibaf insists his actions align with Mojtaba Khamenei’s wishes, yet there is little visible evidence of direct co-ordination. Taken together, these dynamics point to a system that is functioning but not coherently directed. The supreme leader’s authority exists but is not visibly exercised. The presidency is aligned but not leading. Diplomacy is active but not decisive. The military holds key levers without a clear public architect. This is not collapse, but a system struggling to convert leverage—such as the ability to close Hormuz—into clear strategy. It can act across multiple fronts but struggles to signal direction to its own centres of power. For now, the system is holding the line, but it raises the question of whether coherence is being exercised or simply claimed.

    [Original] Three Kosovo Serbs jailed over deadly gun battle and monastery siege
    A court in Kosovo has sentenced two Kosovo Serbs to life imprisonment and a third to 30 years for their roles in a September 2023 attack described as a "terrorist" act. Vladimir Tolić and Blagoj Spasojević received life sentences, while Dušan Maksimović was sentenced to 30 years. They were found guilty of violating Kosovo’s constitutional order and inciting terrorist activities. Prosecutors have charged 45 people in total, but believe most are in Serbia and unlikely to be extradited. The "Banjska incident" marked one of the most dangerous days in Kosovo since its unilateral declaration of independence from Serbia in 2008. A police officer and three members of an armed Kosovo Serb group died during hours of shooting in northern Kosovo. The violence began when police responded to a lorry blockade on a bridge in the early hours of 24 September. Kosovo authorities said about 30 men attacked officers with guns and grenades, killing Sgt Afrim Bunjaku and injuring two colleagues. The armed group retreated to a nearby 14th-century Serbian Orthodox monastery, forced their way in, and barricaded themselves inside, alarming pilgrims from Novi Sad, Serbia. Shooting continued throughout the day, resulting in the deaths of three group members. By the time Kosovo police special forces took control of the monastery in late afternoon, surviving members had escaped despite being surrounded. The self-confessed leader, Kosovo Serb politician Milan Radoičić, later emerged in Serbia, claiming he "personally made all the logistical preparations" for the attack. Police questioned him, but he faces no charges in Serbia and remains at large, though an Interpol arrest warrant limits his travel. Radoičić was deputy leader of the main Kosovo Serb political party, Serbian List, which has strong ties to President Aleksandar Vučić’s Progressive Party. He insisted no Serbian government officials knew of his plans. Kosovo’s acting President Albulena Haxhiu welcomed the verdict, calling it "proof that the attack on the Kosovo police, on the constitutional order and on the security of our country will not remain unpunished." Prime Minister Albin Kurti claimed the attack was part of a Serbian plot to annex majority-Serb northern Kosovo. Relations between Belgrade and Pristina, already strained before the incident, have since been nearly non-existent. Multiple attempts to revive EU-moderated normalization talks have failed. Interior Minister Xhelal Sveçla said "it remains for Serbia to be held accountable for its political, financial and logistical role in this aggression."

    [Original] Israeli police investigate after officers 'cut Palestinian flag' from skullcap
    A British Israeli academic, Alex Sinclair, 53, was detained by Israeli police on Monday for wearing a Jewish kippah embroidered with both an Israeli and a Palestinian flag. Sinclair told the BBC he was sitting in a cafe near his home in Modiin when a religious man confronted him, shouting that the kippah was against the law. Police arrived minutes later, confiscated the kippah, and took Sinclair to the station, where he was frisked, locked in a cell, and denied a phone call. After 20 minutes, he was released, but the kippah was returned with the Palestinian flag cut off. Sinclair called the incident “surreal.” Israeli police stated they responded to a hotline report about a man wearing a kippah with a Palestinian flag. They said Sinclair was brought to the station for clarification and released, adding that a complaint has been filed with the Police Internal Investigations Division, preventing further comment. There is no explicit Israeli law banning the Palestinian flag; courts have viewed it as protected expression, but police can confiscate it if deemed a threat to public order or linked to a terrorist organization. Far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir has instructed a crackdown on Palestinian flags, which rights groups call illegal. Sinclair, a novelist and lecturer in Jewish education at Hebrew University, had worn the kippah for 20 years, describing it as a symbol of “the messy ambivalence of my Jewish-Zionist identity.” He said he chose the design to distinguish himself from right-wing religious nationalists and to express his belief in Israel’s right to exist alongside Palestinian rights. He noted positive reactions from Palestinian citizens of Israel but acknowledged previous “less pleasant moments.” After the incident, he felt “anger and frustration as well as concern” about being on the police radar. Opposition leader Yair Golan criticized the police, calling it “a story about the collapse of the Israeli police.” Hebrew University wrote a strongly-worded letter condemning the “blatant violation of freedom of expression” and the officers’ conduct. Sinclair has filed a complaint for unlawful detention and property damage, seeking compensation for his ruined kippah. He plans to order a new one with both flags, adding, “some people are saying that maybe it’ll start a trend.”
