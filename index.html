    [Original] Hark previews its browser use agent for completing tasks
    Hark, a startup that secured $700 million in Series A funding in May, has launched Hark Handoff, an agent designed to efficiently use browsers to complete tasks. The company asserts that Handoff can navigate websites lacking official APIs, including major platforms such as Target, Walmart, OpenTable, and LinkedIn. According to Hark, the agent analyzes website structure and visual data to determine necessary actions, such as clicking buttons or typing information. The operational premise mirrors previous browser-based agents: users issue commands, and the agent executes tasks ranging from ordering food and coffee to booking travel tickets, filing returns, shopping for essentials, reserving restaurant tables, or conducting research across various sources. In a video demonstration, Hark CEO Brett Adcock showcased the assistant’s ability to build a flower bouquet based on user specifications, including accommodating vague instructions like “some of the florist’s choice.” However, the video displayed only a portion of the process, limiting the ability to fully gauge its effectiveness. For this initial release, Hark is utilizing a post-trained model, with plans to pre-train later this year. The company stated that this approach allows for quicker refinement of its data pipeline, training infrastructure, and techniques. Hark distinguishes its technology from large language models (LLMs) that predict the next token; instead, its model predicts the next action, which could be a click or keyboard input at a specific location. Hark operates in a competitive landscape alongside companies like Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic, as well as VC-funded startups such as Browser Use, Polar, Strawberry, and Aside. Hark claims its Handoff agent is faster than competitors and costs significantly less than other models, including GPT 5.5 and Opus 4.8. The startup has opened a waitlist for its platform and intends to release it by the end of the summer. Analysts warn that this debt burden could trigger "mass layoffs, more aggressive monetization, and other big cost-cutting measures," according to Bloomberg’s Jason Schreier. Christopher Dring of Game Business noted that private equity firms typically adopt a "very hands-on approach," potentially leading to aggressive management. Shams Jorjani of Arrowhead Game Studios expressed concern that the new ownership might prioritize "safe bets" like sequels and mega-franchises over EA’s diverse portfolio, risking a waste of the company’s extensive catalogue. EA’s titles, including The Sims, have long championed inclusivity, raising fears that creative decisions may now be influenced by external political factors. Despite these concerns, EA CEO Andrew Wilson, who will retain his position, stated he is "more energized than ever about the future we are building." The deal ranks as the second-largest in gaming history, following Microsoft’s $69bn purchase of Activision Blizzard. Journalist George Osborn suggests the acquisition is not purely economic but represents a strategic move to own a "soft power asset" entrenched in the sporting community.     [Original] How tech is helping in the battle against wildfires
    Hundreds of thousands of people across France, Spain, and Portugal have been forced to evacuate their homes as devastating wildfires tore through forests and countryside this summer. In France alone, four firefighters have died and 150 others have been injured battling the flames. While the region of Gironde has been the worst hit, the crisis has sparked a debate regarding why humans and 30-year-old aircraft remain essential when drones are utilized in other sectors, such as the war in Ukraine. Chief sergeant Guillaume Millet, a 24-year veteran of the CFDT union’s Fire and Rescue Service branch in Gironde, notes that while firefighting techniques remain largely unchanged, detection technology has evolved significantly. AI-assisted cameras have replaced human lookouts in watch towers, reducing false alarms by distinguishing between smoke and dust from tractors. These systems, installed by companies like Midgard and FireTracking, can detect fires up to 20km away. Jean-Simon Chaudier, CEO of FireTracking, explains that the system alerts firefighters via mobile applications, providing real-time video with 40x zoom, exact fire locations, and simulations of spread based on weather and humidity. Chaudier emphasizes that speed is critical: “If you can help a firefighter to save 15 minutes, it’s the difference between a catastrophe and a fire which can be limited to two or three hectares.” He notes that while a small fire can be extinguished with a glass of water, larger ones require tanks or Canadair amphibious planes. In the Indre-et-Loire département, €1.2m was spent to install 11 detection sites covering 6,000 sq km, addressing the frequent crop fires that plague agricultural land. Looking ahead, the French civil protection service’s Condor programme is testing solar-powered drones with electro-optical cameras, while Spain’s SenForFire project uses sensors to measure gases released before flames become visible. Despite these advancements, Millet asserts that technology cannot replace human firefighters. “It’s not technology that puts out fires; you need people to pull the hoses through the forest to attack them,” he says. Even Canadair planes, which drop 6,000 litres of water, only reduce intensity; ground crews must finish the job. Similarly, Dash planes spraying ammonium phosphate retardant cannot substitute for the physical labor of troops. Innovations continue, with start-up Daidelos seeking investment for heavy-lifting drones capable of spraying 1.2 tonnes of water, and others developing heat-triggered grenade drones for autonomous first response. Researchers are also exploring drones that assess risks like smoke and collapsing structures. However, Millet concludes that AI is “not smart” enough to replace the brawn required for firefighting, a role unchanged since the first modern fire brigade in the Netherlands in the 1670s.

    [Original] What can scientists learn from a stray SpaceX rocket hitting the Moon?
    A stray upper stage of a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket is believed to have crashed into the Moon, creating a new crater near the Einstein crater and providing scientists with a rare research opportunity. The impact occurred on Wednesday at approximately 07:35 BST (06:35 GMT), with the 4,000kg object striking the lunar surface at a speed of about 5,400mph (8,700km/h). The rocket stage, roughly the size of a five-storey building, had been drifting in space since January last year, when it launched from Florida carrying two lunar landers. After completing its mission to propel the landers out of Earth’s orbit, the spent stage was left in a looping trajectory. Over 18 months, gravitational influences from the Earth, Moon, and Sun, along with solar radiation pressure, nudged the debris onto a collision course. Observing the impact proved difficult. The flash was too faint for amateur astronomers, even with high-end telescopes, as it occurred during lunar daylight and lasted only a fraction of a second. A dust plume stretching up to 100km (62 miles) also went largely unseen. South Korea’s Danuri orbiter, which passed near the site shortly before impact, may have captured footage, but its team stated that data would only be released after analysis. NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter is expected to photograph the site in the coming days, allowing for before-and-after comparisons. Powerful telescopes in the Americas, where the sky was dark, may also provide initial data, though processing images of a 14m object hitting a daylight surface from 385,000km away is complex and could take weeks. For planetary geologists, this accidental collision is a scientific gift. Because the rocket’s size, speed, and impact point are known, the event serves as a controlled experiment to test models of crater formation, debris distribution, and lunar tremors. This data will help engineers design safer landers and future Moon bases by understanding debris travel distances and ground shaking. Additionally, material ejected from the surface offers insights into the Moon’s geology and the formation of the Earth-Moon system 4.5 billion years ago. This is not the first human-made object to strike the Moon; since 1959, dozens of spacecraft have crashed there, including the Soviet Luna 2, NASA’s Ranger probes, Apollo rocket stages, and the 2009 LCROSS mission. The last confirmed accidental impact was in 2022, involving a Chinese booster from 2014, highlighting the accumulation of space debris over more than half a century.
