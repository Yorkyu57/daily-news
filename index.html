    [Original] BMS, Novartis, Gilead, Iovance dinged over biologics promos in rare spate of CBER untitled letters
    In a notable escalation of its ongoing crackdown on pharmaceutical marketing, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER) issued four untitled letters on March 9, targeting promotional materials for biologic medicines from Iovance Biotherapeutics, Bristol Myers Squibb, Novartis, and Gilead Sciences’ Kite Pharma. The agency deemed the materials for these cancer therapies “false or misleading,” marking a rare enforcement action from CBER, which had only issued one such letter since 2018 prior to a recent surge in FDA advertising scrutiny. The core violation in all four letters is the inclusion of references to “exploratory analyses” of survival data beyond the specific outcome measures that formed the basis for each drug’s FDA approval. Each letter contains a nearly identical warning that the violations “are particularly concerning from a public health perspective because the promotional materials make misleading representations about [the biologic] being more effective or having greater clinical benefit than has been demonstrated. This may cause doctors and patients to inaccurately weigh the risks versus benefits of treatment with [the biologic], which can be fatal or life-threatening.” The FDA acknowledged company footnotes urging caution but stated these “do not sufficiently mitigate the overall misleading message.” Specifically, Iovance’s letter cites a wide array of materials for its skin cancer therapy Amtagvi—including slide decks, emails, and a website—for referencing overall survival (OS) data. The FDA noted Amtagvi was approved based on objective response rate, with survival studies still ongoing. Bristol Myers Squibb was cited for promotional labeling and a website for its lymphoma treatment Breyanzi that represented OS and progression-free survival (PFS) benefits, despite its approval being based on response rate and duration of response. Novartis’s letter targets materials for its blood cancer drug Kymriah for incorrectly referencing OS and PFS data, as the therapy was approved based on measures like objective response rate, duration of response, and complete remission. Similarly, the letter to Kite Pharma addresses a website and sales aid for the blood cancer therapy Tecartus that refer to PFS, OS, and relapse-free survival benefits, whereas the drug was approved based on response metrics, not survival. The FDA has given each company 15 working days to respond to the concerns. As of the report’s publication, only Gilead had provided comment, confirming receipt of the letter. A Gilead spokesperson stated, “The materials were previously reviewed by the FDA, and Kite followed established regulatory procedures. We take patient safety and compliance seriously and are working to ensure our communications are accurate, balanced, and consistent with the Tecartus prescribing information. We are reviewing the FDA’s feedback and working cooperatively with the Agency to address its comments.”

    [Original] Trustworthy AI in clinical oversight
    The volume and complexity of data in clinical trials are escalating, with Phase III trials now generating an average of 3.6 million data points—three times the volume from a decade ago. Despite the pharmaceutical industry investing $200 billion annually in research and development, drug approvals remain constrained. The core challenge has shifted from data collection to the review, interpretation, and oversight of this vast information. According to Simone Sharma, lead clinical product manager at Revvity Signals, "Traditional manual review can't handle this volume and also simultaneously maintain the rigor that's needed in this kind of highly regulated industry." Artificial intelligence is emerging as a critical tool to accelerate medical and operational review workflows while preserving regulatory rigor. AI-enabled clinical oversight supports automated listing generation and proactive signal detection, helping to identify protocol deviations and data anomalies across millions of data points. "AI-assisted listing generation addresses quite a major bottleneck in the traditional manual review of the workflow," says Sharma, explaining that it automates low-level work so clinical programmers can concentrate on high-value tasks, thereby speeding data access for medical monitors and data managers. A fundamental principle for adoption is a "human in command" approach, where AI augments rather than replaces clinical expertise. Human judgment, contextual interpretation, and regulatory responsibility remain paramount. "Every kind of AI-assisted output...still needs that human on top of it to make the decision," Sharma emphasizes. She notes that teams must monitor AI performance to ensure predictable and consistent behavior, with humans able to intervene and override at any time. This aligns with broader industry efforts, including those by the U.S. FDA's CDER AI Council, which stress the need for built-in guardrails and human oversight. Regulatory frameworks are evolving to accommodate such technologies. The International Council for Harmonisation's (ICH) updated Guideline for Good Clinical Practice E6(R3), finalized in January 2025, reinforces risk-based quality management and continuous oversight, creating a structure where governed AI can support compliance. This shift enables a more targeted review process. "Now, with risk-based and quality monitoring and machine learning algorithms getting better and better, we have the ability to only focus on data points or sites that have been historically problematic," Sharma states, moving away from 100 percent source data verification. Organizations face a choice between building AI solutions internally or adopting purpose-built vendor platforms.
