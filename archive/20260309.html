    [Original] Tech oligarchs reshape humanity while billionaires of old seem quaint
    A striking shift in the composition of the global economic elite underscores the profound influence of a new class of technology oligarchs. In 1992, Bill Gates joined a top ten of billionaires representing a diverse mix of industries and nationalities, with a combined fortune equivalent to about 0.4% of U.S. GDP. By 2025, the list is dominated by tech moguls: Elon Musk, Jeff Bezos, Mark Zuckerberg, Larry Ellison, Steve Ballmer, and Google’s Sergey Brin and Larry Page, alongside only three "old-school" billionaires. Their collective wealth has ballooned to roughly $16 trillion, or about 8% of U.S. GDP. This concentration of wealth and power in the hands of those steering the technological revolution raises a critical question: who decides humanity's future? Fundamental choices about the pursuit of artificial general intelligence, its resource consumption, its impact on labor, and the necessary societal adjustments appear unlikely to be made through public deliberation. Instead, they will be shaped by a tight circle of individuals atop the Forbes list, augmented by figures like Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, OpenAI’s Sam Altman, and investor Peter Thiel. Their shared worldview prioritizes technological solutions to all human challenges, often displaying impatience with democratic governance, especially if it impedes progress toward their vision. They have already signaled a key aspiration by directing nearly $200 million to prevent state-level AI regulations. Their ambitions are explicitly transhumanist. Larry Page has described digital life as the "natural and desirable next step" in cosmic evolution. Sam Altman has stated humanity "will be the first species ever to design our own descendants," while Peter Thiel has arranged for his body to be cryogenically preserved for transfer into an "immortal body." Although not monolithic in detail—some wish to personally inhabit the future, others merely to midwife it—they collectively exhibit a disinterest in mundane human concerns like housing, healthcare, and food prices. Indeed, this oligarchy is offended by the notion that current humans should take precedence over artificial life forms. Altman has argued, "It takes about 20 years of life, and all of the food you consume during that time, before you become [a human] smart," implicitly comparing human and AI training costs. Even at firms like Anthropic, which calls for AI regulation, the goal is a transhuman future, with ethicist Amanda Askell noting AIs will "inevitably form senses of self." While some economists dismiss such concerns as sci-fi, noting history's technological revolutions have ultimately boosted human wellbeing, the current revolution is uniquely unsettling. It is directed by a small, powerful cohort with immense regard for their own preferences, facing little meaningful opposition. Compared to the seemingly harmless billionaires of the past who dealt in packaging, real estate, and retail, today's tech oligarchs are "way scarier" as they aim to transform human civilization at breakneck speed.
