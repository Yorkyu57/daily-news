    [Original] Thousands of people are selling their identities to train AI – but at what cost?
    Silicon Valley's insatiable demand for high-quality, human-grade data has catalyzed a global "data gold rush," creating a thriving industry of marketplaces where individuals sell their identities to train artificial intelligence. As traditional web-scraped datasets like C4 and RefinedWeb face restrictions and researchers predict a data drought by 2026, companies are turning to gig workers to bridge the gap. Platforms such as Kled AI, Silencio, Neon Mobile, Luel AI, and ElevenLabs now pay users to upload videos, photos, voice recordings, and private conversations. The workforce spans diverse economic backgrounds. Jacobus Louw, a 27-year-old in Cape Town, earned $14 for a single video of his neighborhood walk, totaling $50 in weeks to cover groceries. In Ranchi, India, student Sahil Tigga earns over $100 monthly capturing ambient city noise for Silencio. Meanwhile, Ramelio Hill, an 18-year-old welding apprentice in Chicago, sold private phone chats to Neon Mobile for $0.50 per minute, earning hundreds of dollars. For many in developing nations, earning US currency offers stability against devalued local currencies and high unemployment. "As a South African, being paid in USD is more worth it than people think," Louw noted, using the income to fund a spa training course. Experts acknowledge the economic necessity driving this trend. Bouke Klein Teeselink, an economics professor at King's College London, stated that paying for data helps AI firms avoid copyright disputes. Veniamin Veselovsky, an AI researcher, emphasized that "Human data, for now, is the gold standard to sample from outside of the distribution of the model." However, this gig economy carries significant risks. Trainers often grant irrevocable, royalty-free licenses allowing companies to create "derivative works" indefinitely. Mark Graham, a professor at the University of Oxford, warned that "structurally this work is precarious, non-progressive and effectively a dead end," noting that platforms in the global north capture enduring value while workers face a "race to the bottom in wages." Privacy concerns are acute. Hill discovered Neon Mobile went offline after a security flaw exposed user data, leaving him worried about misuse. Jennifer King, a researcher at Stanford, highlighted that consumers risk data being "repurposed in ways that they don't like or didn't understand." While Kled AI founder Avi Patel claims the company vets partners to avoid "questionable intent" and government bodies, the terms often grant "carte blanche" permissions. As the industry grows, workers remain vulnerable to deepfakes, identity theft, and digital exploitation with little legal recourse.

    [Original] Home working, long leases and rise of parking apps - what went wrong for NCP
    National Car Parks (NCP), one of the UK's largest car park operators, has collapsed into administration, placing nearly 700 jobs at risk. The failure of a company charging up to £65 for daily parking has left many baffled. NCP operates 340 sites across airports, train stations, hospitals, and town centres. However, occupancy has plummeted due to the shift toward working from home and the decline in high street shopping in favor of online retail. Nick Stockley, partner at Mayo Wynne Baxter, attributes the collapse to the "combined impact of flexible working, cost-of-living challenges and fuel prices, as well as the general fall in high street shopping and increase in delivery services." Alison Tooze of the British Parking Association (BPA) notes a "big shift" away from five-day commuter parking, with habits becoming more sporadic. NCP's Japanese parent company, Park24, cited higher energy prices following the 2022 Ukraine war and "persistently high" UK inflation as key drivers of increased operating costs. Tooze highlights that maintaining infrastructure, including lighting and staffing, is expensive, while prime locations face high business rates. Furthermore, vehicles are becoming larger and heavier, requiring structural updates. The AA's Edmund King argues that operators failed to expand spaces for modern vehicles and copied each other's "ever-rising ticket prices," leading some drivers to risk fines rather than pay "extortionate" charges. The rise of parking apps offering flexible, app-based alternatives has further eroded NCP's market share. King states that punters have "voted with their wheels," noting NCP failed to keep up with the changing world. Financially, NCP was heavily indebted; as of September 30 last year, debts exceeded assets by £305m. Russ Mould of AJ Bell explains that while car parks are typically asset-backed, interest payments must be met regardless of performance. With falling customer numbers, fixed costs for utilities and staff remained high. Administrators PwC identified a "high concentration" of inflexible, long-term leases that prevented cost reductions or closing unprofitable sites. Park24 noted "significant" rent payments were due. Tooze explains that operators are stuck with assets they cannot easily sublet, creating a "price elasticity tolerance" where raising prices further drives customers away. Michael Lynch of DMH Stallard suggests administrators will seek redundancies and negotiate with landlords regarding "onerous contracts." Potential outcomes include selling the company, divesting assets, or winding up. Stockley predicts profitable airport and station sites will remain, while struggling town centre locations may be sold to residential developers. PwC aims to keep sites open during assessment, meaning drivers will likely experience business as usual for now.
