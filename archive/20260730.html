    [Original] Scientific computing in the age of agentic AI
    A new field report highlights how scientists are leveraging AI coding agents to modernize scientific software, particularly in genomics and other data-intensive fields. Scientific computing remains a cornerstone of modern research, yet the software infrastructure often lags behind rapid data generation. Many widely used tools originated as code accompanying academic papers, created by small teams with limited engineering resources. Consequently, this infrastructure frequently relies on fragile, slow workflows that require constant maintenance, thereby impeding the pace of discovery. AI agents are altering this dynamic by reducing engineering costs and handling tedious implementation tasks. This allows researchers to prototype ideas faster, pursue previously impractical projects, and maintain software more effectively. The report details eight agent-assisted projects in the life sciences: five used Codex alone, while three combined Codex and Claude Code. These case studies range from routine maintenance to large-scale language migrations and GPU-native redesigns. Contributors reported that agents significantly accelerated development, enabling small teams to undertake work that would otherwise demand extensive time or specialized engineering support. However, the report also identifies the persistent challenge of establishing clear, long-term responsibility for these tools. A key finding is the shift in the researcher’s role from implementation to verification and orchestration. Researchers now specify goals, define correctness metrics, and determine when projects are ready for release, retaining control over scientific direction while benefiting from increased velocity. While agents handle well-scoped requests effectively, they cannot reliably judge scientific validity. Agents often express confidence even when errors are present, necessitating human validation. Successful approaches utilized external references or measurable acceptance targets, such as exact output agreement or parity with existing tools. Projects typically proceeded through feedback-driven iterations rather than one-shot approaches, with resolving edge cases and numerical differences consuming the most effort. The report warns that lower implementation costs can lead to fragmented rewrites, spreading expert attention thin. Therefore, long-term stewardship is essential. Some projects, like MHCflurry and cyvcf2, were integrated into original upstream projects, while rustar-aligner moved under new community stewardship after its original project was abandoned. Early coordination with maintainers is recommended, and separate implementations require clear ownership and credible maintenance plans to avoid becoming abandoned code. Ultimately, the case studies suggest that coding agents like Codex significantly lower the costs of maintenance, migration, and optimization. However, their long-term scientific value depends on human decisions regarding what to build, how to verify it, and who will maintain it. The deeper change is not merely the production of more software, but the ability for researchers to focus on defining, validating, and stewarding tools, thereby advancing their fields more efficiently.

    In 2015, during a hiking trip in Hong Kong, BitMEX co-founder Ben Delo and derivatives trader Bavik conceptualized the perpetual swap, a financial instrument that would become one of the most consequential products of the 21st century. BitMEX, founded in 2014 by Delo and Arthur Hayes, initially aimed to serve institutional hedgers with professional infrastructure resembling a Bloomberg terminal. However, the exchange attracted sophisticated retail traders seeking high leverage rather than guaranteed settlement. Despite offering quarterly, monthly, weekly, and even 24-hour futures, customers complained about positions closing unexpectedly. They desired a leveraged product that traded like spot but never expired. Delo proposed a future with no expiry date. Bavik noted this would theoretically be worth infinity due to compounding carrying costs but suggested charging traders the overnight Bitcoin interest rate, similar to LIBOR. Since such a rate did not exist, Delo built it. The perpetual swap launched in May 2016 with little ceremony. It functioned as a futures contract with no expiry, anchored to the spot price through a daily funding rate. Initially, this rate was derived from third-party lending markets like Bitfinex, calculating the cost of holding a long position by subtracting the Bitcoin borrow rate from the dollar borrow rate. As Bitcoin’s price rose through 2016 and 2017, demand for long exposure overwhelmed the mechanism, causing the swap to trade at a persistent premium to spot. The imported interest rate was insufficient to reflect BitMEX’s internal conditions. Delo implemented a dynamic solution: the exchange measured the gap between the swap price and spot price over an eight-hour window, treating it as an implied basis to back-calculate an annualized rate. This rate was then charged at the end of the next eight-hour window. Longs paid shorts, or vice versa, creating a dynamic equilibrium where market makers would arbitrage the price back to spot. BitMEX took no cut from this mechanism. By 2017, BitMEX became the most liquid Bitcoin market, processing $3-4 billion daily. The perpetual swap consolidated liquidity previously spread across multiple contract tenors, resulting in tighter spreads. Competitors eventually copied the model, and every major crypto exchange now uses Delo’s funding rate architecture. Delo estimates the product now generates $40-50 trillion in annual turnover. BitMEX chose not to patent the innovation, opting instead to let the market validate it. Today, traditional finance regulators, including the CFTC, are considering frameworks for perpetual swaps, with speculation that the CME may list them. Delo views this regulatory attention as final validation for the product born from a simple question on a Hong Kong hillside.
