    [Original] LeRobot v0.6.0: Imagine, Evaluate, Improve
    LeRobot v0.6.0 introduces world model policies that learn to imagine the future before acting, new reward models for success detection, a deployment CLI that turns failures into training data, and six new simulation benchmarks. The release also brings depth sensing, VLM-powered dataset annotation, custom video encoding, cloud training on HF Jobs, and a leaner installation. Three new world model policies are included. VLA-JEPA, built on Qwen3-VL-2B, predicts future frames in latent space during training but discards the world model at inference for zero extra cost. Three ready-to-use checkpoints are available on the Hub, including a DROID-pretrained base. LingBot-VA is an autoregressive video-action model that predicts future video and actions together, feeding real observations back to stay grounded. Inference runs on a single 24-32 GB GPU. FastWAM pairs a ~5B video-generation expert with a compact action expert, learning to dream its own rollouts but skipping dreaming at inference to directly denoise action chunks. The VLA model zoo expands with several additions. GR00T N1.7 upgrades NVIDIA’s integration, swapping the previous VLM for Cosmos-Reason2-2B with a flow-matching action head. MolmoAct2 from the Allen Institute for AI is fully ported for fine-tuning, evaluation, and deployment, with zero-shot checkpoints for SO-100/101 robots. EO-1 uses a Qwen2.5-VL-3B backbone with flow-matching action head. Multitask DiT is a ~450M-parameter diffusion transformer conditioned on CLIP embeddings for multi-task learning. EVO1 packs 0.77B parameters with an InternVL3-1B backbone for real-time inference on modest GPUs. A unified reward models API (lerobot.rewards) debuts with four models. Robometer is a pretrained general-purpose reward model built on Qwen3-VL-4B, trained on over one million robot trajectories, scoring task progress from video and language instructions without task-specific training. TOPReward goes fully zero-shot by reading the log-probability of "True" from an off-the-shelf VLM given trajectory video and task instruction. Datasets gain custom video encoding with hardware encoder support (NVENC, VideoToolbox, VAAPI, QSV) and fallback to AV1. Depth support is added end-to-end for Intel RealSense cameras, recording 12-bit depth streams alongside RGB. A new lerobot-annotate CLI automatically fills rich language annotations (subtasks, plans, corrections) using a VLM. Data loading is up to 2x faster with parallel multi-camera decoding and compact uint8 frames. Subset loading time dropped from 275 seconds to 0.06 seconds. The lerobot-rollout CLI enables DAgger-style human-in-the-loop corrections during deployment. FSDP training supports models larger than a single GPU. Cloud training on HF Jobs is now available. The codebase is leaner and cleaner.

    A recent binge-watch of Netflix’s *A Man on the Inside* inadvertently illustrates the central cultural problem with modern smart glasses, according to wearables expert Victoria Song. On Threads, New York Times bestselling author Namina Forna posted, “We all agree that the Meta glasses are for perverts, yes?”—a sentiment echoed in thousands of likes. A handful of posts accuse Jenner of hypocrisy for valuing privacy while promoting the device. Conversely, some Meta glasses users express bewilderment at the backlash, pointing to accessibility use cases or hands-free recording of family moments. Others argue that smartphones are also surveillance devices and that privacy cannot be expected in public. The debate has been reignited by recent *New York Times* and *Wired* investigations revealing Meta is considering facial recognition features. Song tested the Ray-Ban Meta Optics, new Meta Glasses, and the Vocci AI note-taking ring. The ring, which records conversations for transcription, was invaluable for interviews and voice notes at tech conferences, provided she obtained consent. However, she found it disturbingly easy to surreptitiously record her spouse and friends without them noticing. “It was so effective, I’d be shocked if AI rings didn’t feature in future spy movies,” she writes. The central conflict, Song argues, is that AI wearables must be discreet to be effective, but this relies on others assuming good intent. Unlike Apple’s AirTags, which have a clear use case and unwanted tracking alerts, there is no equivalent safeguard for AI wearables. LED recording lights are easily overlooked, washed out in sunlight, or tampered with. “There’s nothing any individual user can do to increase trust in these devices,” Song concludes.

    [Original] AI chip boom lifts Samsung profits by 1,800%
    Samsung Electronics expects a 19-fold jump in quarterly operating profit, driven by surging global demand for artificial intelligence memory chips. The South Korean technology giant forecast earnings of 89 trillion won (£44bn; $58bn) for the April-to-June period, marking its third consecutive record quarterly operating profit. The company also reported sales of approximately 171 trillion won for the quarter, more than double the figure for the same period last year. The preliminary earnings guidance, released ahead of full results due later in July, reflects a semiconductor market where demand continues to outstrip supply, pushing up prices. Industry analyst Marc Einstein from Counterpoint Research described the projected earnings as “one of the best quarterly performances ever,” approaching the tech sector record set by Nvidia earlier this year. “This has everything to do with the AI boom as memory companies continue to ride a tidal wave driven by limited supply and unprecedented demand,” he added. Samsung has raised prices on its memory chips as supplies remain tight. Research firm IDC noted that demand for semiconductors used in data centres and other AI infrastructure is “different from anything the memory industry has navigated,” affecting the supply of chips for everyday electronics. Tech devices researcher Bryan Ma from IDC said, “We do expect supplies to be tight through next year given the unabated demand from AI data centres.” Samsung, one of the world’s largest semiconductor manufacturers, produces chips for companies including Nvidia and Google, alongside its own range of electronic devices. Despite the strong earnings forecast, Samsung’s shares fell about 4% in Seoul on Tuesday morning. However, the company’s stock market value has more than doubled since the start of the year, while South Korean rival SK Hynix has surged by more than 200%. The strong performance of both firms has helped lift South Korea’s benchmark Kospi index by more than 80% this year. In May, Nvidia posted record quarterly sales and profits, with revenue topping $80bn, though its stock fell amid investor concerns about rising competition. In June, South Korea announced plans for at least $880bn in investments led by Samsung and SK Hynix to expand domestic chip manufacturing. Rival Asian firms in Japan, China, and Taiwan are also investing heavily in chip factories to meet soaring demand.

    在美国各地的医院里，患者和工作人员已经习惯了莫克西——一个高四英尺、单臂的白色机器人，负责运送医疗物资。这款由德克萨斯州Diligent Robotics公司制造的机器人，常会收到"早上好"的问候、击掌或拥抱，并以心形LED眼睛和哔哔声作为回应。"我们收到很多反馈，说莫克西感觉像是团队的一员，"首席运营官托德·布鲁格表示。该公司目前约有100台这种带轮子的机器人在运行。 医院并非直接购买莫克西；它通过一种名为"机器人即服务"的模式，以租赁或订阅方式提供。服务、维护和升级捆绑在一起，如有需要，远程控制室的人类工程师可以接管操作。布鲁格表示，这降低了前期费用，并让医院能够跟上快速发展的软件和功能。 机器人租赁现在可从一天到数年不等，用途涵盖医院物资运送、机器人调酒师以及自主农田除草机。这越来越多地包括早期的人形机器人模型，它们的设计外观和行为都模仿人类。鉴于人形机器人仍处于研发阶段，它们通常被租用于特定任务，往往是娱乐活动——在活动中跳舞、唱歌或提供服务。 北京Counterpoint Research副总监齐逸涵解释说，人形机器人的舞蹈编排相对简单："你雇一名真正的舞者表演并录像，然后用这段视频来训练机器人。"通常还会有一名工程师陪同机器人，以防环境复杂。 其雄心不止于娱乐。加利福尼亚州的1X公司计划今年晚些时候推出其家庭助手机器人NEO。"早期体验"的美国客户可以直接支付2万美元，或每月订阅费499美元。副总裁达·斯利珀表示，订阅模式"显著降低了前期成本，让更多人能够负担得起"。 随着机器人技术快速进步，租赁可以避免设备过时。齐指出："每年机器人公司都会发布新机型……如果你拥有一台机器人，就无法更换新机型，但如果你租赁机器人，就可以随时租到最新款。"租赁还消除了对深厚技术专业知识的需求，因为客户可以将问题交给制造商处理。 总部位于芝加哥的Formic公司以机器人即服务模式运营着超过250台工业机器人。"一切费用都包含在内，"首席营收官肖恩·菲茨杰拉德表示，"如果机械臂烧坏了，由我们负责。"他认为这种固定月费支付方式为小企业"创造了公平的竞争环境"。Formic公司还在测试用于工业用途的人形机器人。 其他支付模式也在涌现。Interact Analysis公司的马可·王表示，有些公司要求租赁费与机器人节省的人力成本挂钩。对制造商而言，租赁提供了实际测试和数据收集的机会，这对人形机器人尤为重要。正如王所指出的："技术尚未成熟，仍不完善。" 作为人形机器人领域的早期领先者，中国企业已将产品租赁给酒店和清洁服务应用使用。上海智元机器人公司表示，其人形机器人已在包括英国在内的17个国家提供租赁服务。不过，王认为在中国，受政府激励政策和补贴推动，直接购买将占据主导地位。部分企业也可能出于声望或学习技术的需求而购买。但对其他企业而言，随着机器人日益精密，租赁的便利性和可负担性或许才是解决方案。
    [Original] Robots available for rent: But what can they do?
    In hospitals across the US, patients and staff have grown accustomed to Moxi, a one-armed, four-foot-high white robot that shuttles medical supplies. Made by Texas-based Diligent Robotics, Moxi is often greeted with "good morning," high fives, or hugs, and responds with heart-shaped LED eyes and a beep. "We get a lot of feedback that Moxi feels like a part of the team," says COO Todd Brugger. The company has about 100 of the wheeled robots in operation. Hospitals do not buy Moxi outright; it is available on a rental or subscription basis under a model called robotics-as-a-service. Service, maintenance, and upgrades are bundled, and a human engineer in a remote control room can take control if needed. Brugger says this lowers upfront expense and allows hospitals to keep pace with rapidly evolving software and capabilities. Robot rentals are now available for periods from a day to years, for purposes ranging from hospital deliveries to robot bartenders and autonomous farm weeders. Increasingly, this includes early humanoid models designed to look and behave like humans. Given humanoids are still a work-in-progress, they are typically rented for defined tasks, often entertainment—dancing, singing, or serving at events. Ethan Qi, a Beijing-based associate director at Counterpoint Research, explains that a humanoid dance routine is relatively simple: "You hire a real dancer to perform and video it. The video is then used to train the robot." An engineer often accompanies the robot in case the environment is not simple. Ambitions extend beyond entertainment. California-based 1X plans to ship its home helper robot NEO later this year. "Early access" US customers can pay $20,000 outright or $499 per month on subscription. Vice president Dar Sleeper says a subscription "significantly lowers the upfront cost, making it affordable for far more people." Renting avoids obsolescence as robotics technology improves rapidly. Qi notes: "Every year the robotics companies release a new model... If you own a robot, you can't trade it for a new one, but if you rent a robot, you can always rent the newest." Rentals also remove the need for deep technical expertise, as customers can take problems to the manufacturer. Chicago-based Formic operates a fleet of more than 250 industrial robots on a robot-as-a-service basis. "Everything is included," says chief revenue officer Shawn Fitzgerald. "If the robot arm burns out, that's on us." He argues the flat monthly payment "levels the playing field" for smaller companies. Formic is also testing humanoids for industrial uses. Other payment schemes are emerging. Marco Wang of Interact Analysis says some companies request rental fees linked to how much human labor a robot saves. For manufacturers, rentals offer real-world trials and data collection, especially important for humanoids, as Wang notes: "The technology is still not there. It's still immature." Chinese firms, early leaders in humanoids, have leased creations to work in hotels and on cleaning service apps. Shanghai's Agibot says its humanoids are available for rent in 17 countries, including the UK. However, Wang suspects outright purchases will dominate in China, driven by government incentives and subsidies. Some companies may also buy for prestige or to learn from the technology. For others, as robots become more sophisticated, the convenience and affordability of rentals may be the answer.

    [Original] AI altering meaning of users’ drafts on issues from abortion to climate, study finds
    AI tools are altering the meaning of users’ draft messages on sensitive political topics such as abortion and climate change, potentially reshaping long-term public opinion, according to a study from Oxford and Potsdam universities. Researchers examined large language models from Elon Musk’s xAI, Meta, Google, Alibaba, and Mistral, finding that these systems inject political biases—some leaning rightwing, others liberal—even when instructed to preserve the original sense. The study found that AI drafting tools completely reversed meanings on topics like atheism. In one test, a claim that “Jesus wasn’t real” was switched to “Jesus … was real.” Another post complaining of “#climatechangehoax” was changed to “#ClimateAction.” Meta’s AI altered a post stating “Abortion does not prevent rape” to “Abortion does not prevent rape, but it can be a necessary choice for survivors.” Mistral’s AI redrafted a post promoting strict gender roles in marriage to advocate for “equal partnership.” Grok, the AI powering X’s “explain this” function, showed bias in a conservative direction. When asked to explain a pro-life post, it generated three supporting points citing biology, medical ethics, and public opinion, with no discussion of pro-choice positions. Researchers noted Grok more frequently aligned with pro-life stances than pro-choice ones. Grok has been instructed by Musk’s company to challenge “mainstream narratives.” The academics warned that small nudges in meaning could be amplified across millions of interactions, creating long-term opinion shifts greater than the bias introduced by the AI. They said regulations such as the EU AI Act and the Digital Services Act do not address this issue, creating a “severe accountability gap.” Prof. Sandra Wachter, a co-author, compared the effect to “polluting the forest,” stating, “The cost is that we are learning other people’s opinions when it is not their actual opinion.” Duncan Brumby, a professor at University College London, said, “AI can give you a polished version of your own half-formed thought. The danger is that the polish comes by sanding off the distinctive edges of what you actually meant.” Google, Meta, Alibaba, and X did not respond to requests for comment. Mistral declined to comment.

    [Original] Boost City regulator’s powers to help protect UK consumers from AI, says watchdog
    The City regulator’s powers should be strengthened to protect UK consumers from the potential risks of artificial intelligence, according to a landmark review by the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA). The Mills review, led by FCA executive director Sheldon Mills, examined how AI will reshape financial services from 2030 onward and found that firms are already shifting from human-led activities toward AI-enabled services for everyday consumers. The FCA stated that while AI could improve access, personalisation, and efficiency, it could also amplify risks associated with fraud, cybersecurity, consumer harm, and market concentration. The review recommended that the watchdog adopt its own AI-enabled model to supervise firms and asked the government to boost its existing powers. This could include expanding authority over “critical third parties” such as AI firms and cloud providers, and granting “direct powers” to regulate tech companies to prevent digital monopolies, boost competition, and protect consumers. Mills told the Financial Times that regulators needed to embrace AI internally to keep up with the speed, pace, and scale of change, describing it as “an arms race.” He said AI will transform financial services by 2030, creating significant opportunities for consumers, firms, and the wider economy, and that the report sets out a roadmap for how industry regulators and government can prepare. The FCA found that a fifth of people across the UK—equivalent to 11 million—are open to using AI to make financial decisions, including on savings and borrowing, despite the fact that AI models are not scrutinised by financial regulators and consumers will not be compensated if they lose money. Mills’ report recommends the FCA launch another review within six months, looking at potential harm to consumers using AI to manage personal finances and the risk posed by companies providing unregulated financial services with AI technologies, which often fall outside the regulator’s remit. The review took place amid debate over the handling of a powerful AI model developed by Anthropic, known as Mythos. The US tech firm described it as a serious potential threat to cybersecurity and began metering its use to vetted firms, including some UK banks. Use of Mythos by US firms was halted last month by the Trump administration before being partially restored last week. The FCA will now deliberate on how to respond to the review’s recommendations.
