    More Recalls, Market Withdrawals, & Safety Alerts Foods Alive Inc of Angola, IN, is recalling its 8-ounce packages of organic moringa leaf powder because they have the potential to be contaminated with Salmonella, an organism which can cause serious and sometimes fatal infections in young children, frail or elderly people, and others with weakened immune systems. Healthy persons infected with Salmonella often experience fever, diarrhea (which may be bloody), nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain. In rare circumstances, infection with Salmonella can result in the organism getting into the bloodstream and producing more severe illnesses such as arterial infections (i.e., infected aneurysms), endocarditis and arthritis. The recalled organic moringa leaf powder was distributed nationwide in retail stores and through online sellers including but not limited to www.foodsalive.com. The product comes in an 8 ounce stand up pouch with a freshest by date of 110127 and the number 02726 below the date, and were shipped between January 27th and August 7th 2026. No illnesses have been reported to date in connection with this problem. The potential for contamination was found after our supplier notified us that a sample of their super green powder mix, collected at retail, tested positive for Salmonella. The source of the problem has not been determined. The company has suspended production and distribution of these products while FDA and the company continue their investigation. Consumers who have purchased 8 ounce packages of organic moringa leaf powder are urged to return them to the place of purchase for a full refund. Consumers with questions may contact the company at 260-488-4497.

    [Original] The sameness problem behind those unappetizing AI-generated menus
    Generative AI has permeated the restaurant industry, resulting in menus that feature illustrations which are eerily flawless, precisely symmetrical, and oddly smooth. This phenomenon, described by TechCrunch senior writer Amanda Silberling, stems from models trained on a narrow, pleasing aesthetic that elicits a visceral sense of wrongness in consumers. While some AI-generated images are egregiously fake—such as burritos with cheese that resembles avant-garde art—others are so ordinary that the artificiality is only noticeable upon close inspection. Alex Lisle, CTO of Reality Defender, a startup specializing in AI-detection and content-verification tools, compared the effect to an alien attempting to make a pizza without understanding its core principles. The specific aesthetic of these menus, where ice cream scoops are perfectly round and shrimp appear to eat their own tails, is explained by the training methods of large language models and diffusion models. These models, which power tools like ChatGPT and Midjourney, identify patterns in vast datasets to predict user requests. Lisle noted that much of this output resembles a Chili’s menu from 2015 because that was the corpus from which the models drew their function. As AI-generated content seeps into training datasets, models risk "model collapse," a process Lisle likened to mad cow disease, where feeding outputs back into the model causes inbreeding and eventual failure. A less extreme form, convergence, degrades quality without rendering the AI entirely useless. Because fast-food menus from chains like Wendy’s, Burger King, and McDonald’s share similar styles, AI outputs mimic this homogenized look. Lee Rainie, director of the Imagining the Digital Future Center at Elon University, explained that dataset optimization for pleasingness leads to homogenization, as AI tends to "shave off the edges." This smoothing effect is exacerbated when users repeatedly edit AI-generated menus; a user named Labtec demonstrated on X that editing a ChatGPT menu 100 times resulted in food images becoming progressively less realistic, a result TechCrunch replicated. Rainie noted that people possess an unarticulated sensibility for detecting AI-generated content, contributing to the pronounced backlash against restaurants using such menus. Scientific research supports this aversion. Researchers at the University of Duisburg-Essen in Germany found that AI-generated food images exhibit an uncanny valley effect, where images that look almost real elicit more disgust than obviously fake ones. Lisle warned that this shift undermines the traditional reliance on visual evidence in court systems, stating, "The world has fundamentally shifted, for good or for ill." Despite the negative consumer reaction, the issues surrounding these "perfectly browned hamburger buns" extend beyond the dinner table, impacting broader perceptions of authenticity in a digital age.
