  • 蛋奶冻味水果的兴起
    阿肖卡·希瓦雷迪来自印度南部科拉尔地区一个农民家庭，该地区干旱频发，年降雨量仅60至70厘米。"农民们要挖深达1300英尺的管井——他们的大部分钱都花在了找水上，"他说。在持续亏损后，全家于2005年放弃务农，搬到班加罗尔开了一家蔬菜店。希瓦雷迪后来成为了一名人工智能软件工程师，但从未失去对农业的兴趣。2018年，他以科学方法重振家族农场，寻找一种能在极少用水且无需大量使用农药的情况下存活的作物。 他选择了释迦果，这是一种表面凹凸不平、大小如大号牛油果的水果，果肉呈奶油状且味道甘甜。这种树在他的家乡野生生长，当地人采摘并出售果实。为了最大化产量，希瓦雷迪将树木种得更密，并选择了三种具有不同优势的品种。"去年我产了大约20吨。今年大约25吨。印度国内外对释迦果的需求量很大，"他说。 传统释迦果品种面临挑战。巴兰加尔品种的保质期只有三到四天，且籽很多。"传统品种风味极佳，但存在果肉含量低、籽多、保质期极短的问题，"班加罗尔印度园艺研究所首席科学家萨克蒂维尔·T博士说。他的团队培育了一种名为阿尔卡·萨汉的杂交品种，该品种在室温下可保鲜一周，果肉更多、籽更少。"从野生品种30%的出肉率到阿尔卡·萨汉等杂交品种70%的出肉率，这一转变实际上使农民无需更多土地就能将可用收成翻倍，"萨克蒂维尔说。印度园艺研究所目前正在研究如何防止释迦果果肉在提取后变褐。 马哈拉施特拉邦是印度最大的释迦果产地，产量占全国近三分之一。农民纳夫纳特·马尔哈里·卡斯帕特花费数十年时间收集种子并进行异花授粉。"培育一个新品种需要12到15年。这不是一项快速的工作——而是数十年的实验，"他说。他的NMK-01品种以高产著称，于2014年上市。"我们现在在近50英亩的土地上种植释迦果，每英亩产量约10吨。这个改良品种为出口创造了机会。我们开始向海湾国家出口，甚至运往欧洲，"他说。 出口商马诺杰·库马尔·巴赖将NMK-01品种运往美国、阿联酋、沙特阿拉伯和欧洲。"出口时我们更倾向于NMK-01品种，因为它保质期更长、果皮更厚、果肉更多、味道更甜，"他说。出口这种娇嫩的水果需要精确规划。"释迦果对高温非常敏感，即使短暂暴露也会缩短保质期，"他说。公路运输在夜间进行以避免高温，水果在通过冷藏车和空运运输前会预冷五小时。现在更多水果以果肉或粉末形式出口，储存于零下18摄氏度，这比空运更便宜且减少浪费。 回到科拉尔，希瓦雷迪计划建立一个果肉加工厂来处理未售出的作物，但设备投资巨大。"释迦果处于一个奇怪的空白地带。需求在增长，但由于这种作物天生耐寒，种植并未实现高科技化。它生长在贫瘠的土壤中，需水量极少，靠降雨就能存活。农民不需要昂贵的灌溉系统、传感器或受控环境，因此技术采用率仍然很低，"他说。
    [Original] The rise of the fruit that tastes like custard
    Ashoka Shivareddy comes from a family of farmers in the drought-prone Kolar district of southern India, where rainfall is only 60 to 70 centimetres annually. "Farmers dig borewells of up to 1,300 feet - most of their money goes into chasing water," he says. After mounting losses, the family gave up farming in 2005 and moved to Bengaluru, starting a vegetable shop. Shivareddy became an AI software engineer but never lost his interest in farming. In 2018, he revived the family farm with a scientific approach, seeking a crop that could survive with very little water and without heavy pesticide use. He chose custard apple, a knobbly fruit the size of a large avocado with creamy, sweet flesh. The trees grow wild in his area, and locals harvest and sell the fruit. To maximize yield, Shivareddy planted trees closer together and selected three varieties with different benefits. "Last year I produced around 20 tonnes. This year, it's about 25 tonnes. There is huge demand for custard apple in India and abroad," he says. Traditional custard apple varieties face challenges. The Balangar variety has a shelf life of just three to four days and many seeds. "Traditional varieties have excellent flavour, but they suffer from low pulp content, high seed count, and a very poor shelf life," says Dr Sakthivel T, principal scientist at the Indian Institute of Horticulture Research (IIHR) in Bangalore. His team developed a hybrid named Arka Sahan, which lasts a week at room temperature and has more pulp and fewer seeds. "The shift from 30% pulp recovery in wild varieties to 70% recovery in hybrids like Arka Sahan has effectively doubled the usable harvest for farmers without needing more land," Sakthivel says. IIHR is now researching ways to prevent custard apple pulp from turning brown after extraction. Maharashtra is India's leading custard apple producer, accounting for nearly a third of national output. Farmer Navnath Malhari Kaspate spent decades collecting seeds and cross-pollinating them. "It takes 12 to 15 years to develop a new variety. This is not quick work - it's decades of experimentation," he says. His NMK-01 variety, known for high yields, went on sale in 2014. "We now grow custard apple on nearly 50 acres, with yields of about 10 tonnes per acre. This improved variety has created opportunity for exports. We started exporting to Gulf countries, and even sent it to Europe," he says. Exporter Manoj Kumar Barai ships the NMK-01 variety to the US, UAE, Saudi Arabia and Europe. "For exports we prefer the NMK-01 variety because it has better shelf life, thicker skin, more pulp, and a sweeter taste," he says. Exporting the delicate fruit requires precise planning. "Custard apple is highly sensitive to heat, and even short exposure can reduce its shelf life," he says. Road journeys are done overnight to avoid heat, and the fruit is pre-cooled for five hours before being transported in refrigerated vans and air freighted. More fruit is now exported as pulp or powder, stored at -18C, which is cheaper than air freight and reduces waste. Back in Kolar, Shivareddy plans to set up a pulp processing unit to use unsold crop, but the investment in equipment is significant. "Custard apple sits in a strange gap. Demand is rising, but the farming hasn't gone high-tech as the crop is naturally hardy. It grows in poor soil, needs very little water, and survives on rainfall. Farmers don't need expensive irrigation, sensors, or controlled environments so tech adoption stays low," he says.

    [Original] Rachel Reeves tells ministers to ‘buy British’ in four key industries
    Chancellor Rachel Reeves has instructed cabinet ministers to award government contracts in four critical industries—shipbuilding, steel-making, energy, and artificial intelligence—directly to British companies, expressing disappointment that too much government business is being sent abroad. In a letter co-signed by Cabinet Office minister Chris Ward and seen by the Guardian, Reeves wrote: “We should all want to see more businesses grow and prosper and create good, skilled jobs and apprenticeships here in Britain. Every secretary of state can and must lead this agenda within their departments if we are to deliver the change the public expect.” She added: “Therefore, it is disappointing that we are still seeing too many government contract awards where this is not happening. We have instructed officials to take further steps to ensure your departments act in the wider national interest rather than solely focusing on narrow operational priorities.” Treasury and Cabinet Office officials will now monitor billions of pounds’ worth of contracts and may override decisions by departmental ministers. Reeves is understood to be extremely unhappy about several high-profile contracts awarded to foreign bidders or put to open tender, including a £200m contract for navy support vessels awarded to Dutch shipbuilder Damen, a £9m deal to refit the research ship David Attenborough signed with Danish shipyard Orskov, and a potential £1.9bn contract to upgrade the Faslane shipyard, which services Britain’s nuclear submarines. Louise Gilmour, secretary of GMB Scotland, said of the Faslane contract: “It is hard to imagine another country in the world being so willing to send such work abroad … it is beyond time the MoD started defending our workers and their communities.” However, some in government argue competitive bidding secures the best products at the cheapest price, and direct awards could push costs up for taxpayers. UK Research and Innovation defended the Orskov deal as the result of an extensive competitive process. Reeves’s allies say her irritation is directed at civil service “inertia” in following rules that prioritize cost over nationality, which risks undermining national security. In March, Ward said the four sectors would be recognized as critical for national security, allowing direct contract awards. Under the 2023 Procurement Act, ministers can ban companies deemed a national security risk. Reeves promised further guidelines for accounting officers to consider contractors’ nationality and warned: “We look forward to confirmation that your departments are complying with the new guidance when it is issued later this summer. Each department will be held to account.” A government spokesperson said: “For the first time, procurement of shipbuilding, along with steel, AI and energy infrastructure, will be recognised as critical for national security.
