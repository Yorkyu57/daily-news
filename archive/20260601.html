    [Original] Put a £5 deposit on vapes to stop littering, say waste companies
    Waste companies have called for a deposit of up to £5 to be charged on vapes to encourage proper disposal, a year after disposable vapes were banned partly due to fire risks in bin lorries and waste facilities. The Environmental Services Association (ESA), the industry body for waste firms, argues that vape recycling has not improved enough and proposes a refundable deposit at the point of purchase as a "simple, fair, efficient and cost-neutral solution." Under the plan, customers would get the deposit back when they return the vape to a store or recycling facility. Biffa, the UK’s largest waste company, suggested the £5 figure, which would be subject to consultation. Patrick Brighty, ESA’s head of recycling policy, said: "Despite the ban, each week operators across the waste sector continue to see hundreds of thousands of carelessly discarded vapes arrive at their facilities hidden among other waste, which poses a major fire risk." He added that vapes discarded with other rubbish are "unlikely to be recycled, which is a chronic waste of the precious materials they contain," and that existing take-back infrastructure is "currently underperforming" due to a lack of incentives. The Local Government Association (LGA) reports that the number of vapes thrown away weekly has fallen from 8.2 million to about six million since the ban. However, some firms now sell reusable vapes similar in size and price to disposables, with USB ports and reusable tanks. The LGA has called for these to be banned. Councillor Dr Wendy Taylor, chairwoman of the LGA’s health and wellbeing committee, said: "A year on, the volume of vapes in our bins has dropped, but industry has moved faster than regulation – the products causing fires in our bin lorries today are effectively the same disposables in a different shell." She urged that year two of the ban focus on enforcement and closing this "loophole." Marcus Saxton, chairman of the Independent British Vape Trade Association, conceded "more to be done" on recycling but warned a deposit scheme would drive consumers to illicit retailers who do not charge it. "Those retailers that aren’t abiding by their legal obligation won’t do this, it won’t be enforced, and ultimately consumers will go to that route to purchase which is a complete disaster," he said, noting that many illicit retailers already source from the black market. The Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs did not directly respond to questions about a deposit. Environment Secretary Emma Reynolds said the government had taken "decisive action" and is "committed to going further" by holding retailers to account if they lack vape recycling bins.

Morten Abildstrøm, a Danish Environmental Agency official on the island of Anholt, told daily newspaper Jyllands Posten that the whale would be cut into pieces after veterinarians and researchers had travelled there to take samples. Those pieces will then be taken elsewhere to be destroyed, he said. The whale was discovered off Anholt's shoreline earlier in May, two weeks after a private mission to save the humpback from being stranded on Germany's Baltic Sea coast ended in failure. German authorities had given the go-ahead for that attempt despite viewing it as a long shot, as the whale was already severely weakened from its ordeal, having acquired damage to its skin from the lower salt content of the Baltic Sea. The mission used inflatable cushions to pull it to the surface before dragging it back to the North Sea on a floating platform. Exactly how the whale ended up on an island off Denmark's East Jutland coast remains unclear. The barge that carried the mammal away from Germany released it into the sea about 70km (45 miles) from the northern tip of Denmark. The urgency in disposing of the body was because the whale was now sitting on a popular beach, according to Abildstrøm. Officials previously attempted to move the carcass from the beach to the port of Grenaa on the Danish mainland, but this proved unsuccessful due to poor weather conditions. The saga has transfixed Germans since it began, and has left islanders on Anholt bemused by the continued interest. "Timmy" first became stranded in Lübeck Bay, on Germany's northern coastline, after apparently becoming entangled in netting.
