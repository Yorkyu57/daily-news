    Argentina's wine capital, Mendoza, relies heavily on fresh water from the Andes mountains to irrigate crops and sustain communities. Virginia de Valle, a winemaker in the region, warns that recent government reforms loosening glacier protections threaten this vital supply. "Without water, there would be no wine," De Valle states, noting that the Andes' winter snow and glaciers feed rivers essential for agriculture and domestic consumption. She emphasizes that "Mendoza is the daughter of water," particularly as glaciers help mitigate increasingly common droughts. Argentina was the first nation to enact specific glacier protection laws in 2010, designating them as vital water reserves and prohibiting damaging commercial activities. This legislation also safeguarded periglacial environments, including permafrost, with glaciers recorded in a national inventory by the Argentine Institute of Snow Research, Glaciology and Environmental Sciences (Ianigla). However, newly passed reforms shift the authority to provincial governments to determine if glaciers are of "strategic importance" regarding water, biodiversity, or tourism. Provinces can now remove glaciers from the national inventory, stripping them of federal protections if deemed non-strategic. Proponents argue the 2010 law hindered necessary extraction projects. President Javier Milei views the Andes as key to unlocking billions in mining investment, stating, "Argentina doesn't export even a single gram of copper, while Chile... exports $20bn a year." Mining executives from Glencore, Lundin, and BHP Group have visited Milei, with Bloomberg reporting potential investments of around $40bn in Argentina's copper industry. Supporters claim this development is crucial for the country's energy transition and economic growth. Conversely, opponents fear large-scale mining will alter river flows and threaten water security for seven million people across 12 provinces. Over 100,000 citizens signed up for public hearings, though fewer than 400 could speak. Agostina Rossi Serra of Greenpeace notes, "It made clear that... it was the people, the public, who were asking for water to continue to be protected." The campaign slogan "Los glaciares no se tocan" (hands off the glaciers) has appeared nationwide. Glaciologist Lucas Ruiz calls the reforms based on "false arguments," asserting that "If it's a glacier, it has ice and contributes water." He highlights a "stark paradox": while energy transition requires copper and lithium, melting glaciers threaten water security, and preventing this melt requires reducing carbon footprints. Ruiz insists any mining must be responsible with thorough impact assessments. Greenpeace fears financial incentives will lead provinces to lower environmental standards. Meanwhile, lawyer Federico Palavecino argues provinces should decide their own protection levels to secure vital cash for communities. De Valle concludes that while the reforms affect wineries, "first, it will affect life."
